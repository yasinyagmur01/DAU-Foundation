RESEARCH REQUEST: WHEN AN OUTCOME IS WRITTEN BY BOTH AN INDIVIDUAL PATH AND A
GROUP-WIDE SIMULTANEOUS SHOCK, HOW IS THE OUTCOME DEFINED AND DECOMPOSED

1. THE SYSTEM, IN THE MINIMUM DETAIL NEEDED

We run a simulation study of inheritance across generations. It has the
structure of a controlled experiment with three arms.

- Each arm has its own population of N = 8 agents and its own shared resource
  pool. The arms never interact.
- Each agent lives a sequence of discrete events. At each event it announces
  a withdrawal from the shared pool, receives what the pool can deliver, and
  its internal state is updated.
- At the end of a generation, agents are ranked by a fitness quantity,
  winners of a size-2 tournament produce heirs, population size stays fixed
  at N, and the number of heirs per parent is the selection weight w. w can
  be 0, 1, 2 and so on.
- G = 3 generations, so there are G - 1 = 2 selection episodes per seed.
- Everything is deterministic: the same seed reproduces the same run
  bit-for-bit. Different seeds are allowed and normal (an earlier study of
  ours ran 40 of them). The determinism requirement is about reproducibility,
  not about running only one seed.

The outcome variable, which we call z, is the magnitude of a permanent
internal shift ("drift") carried by an agent, read at a FIXED event ordinal
in every life (event number 10), so that all agents are read at the same age.

Selection is measured with the Price equation: the covariance between w and z
across the agents of one generation, plus the transmission term.


2. THE PROBLEM, STATED PRECISELY

The variable z is written by exactly one internal function, and that function
fires on exactly one condition: an incoming magnitude at or above a fixed
threshold. But that function has TWO callers.

Path A, INDIVIDUAL. The agent's own surprise. Each agent computes a
prediction error from its own experience; when the associated magnitude
crosses the threshold, that agent's drift is updated. This path is
agent-specific: two agents in the same arm can and do differ on it.

Path B, COMMON SHOCK. When the shared pool falls below a fixed fraction of
its capacity, a resource "crisis" fires. The crisis applies a fixed magnitude
to EVERY agent of that arm at the SAME event. The magnitude is a constant, so
every agent receives an identical increment, at the same moment, in the same
internal domain.

Measured consequences, from a run of 216 lives across four seeds:

- In a seed where the pool never entered crisis, the 8 agents of a cell
  showed 5 distinct values of z.
- In seeds where the crisis fired, the same cell showed 1 or 2 distinct
  values of z.
- In one seed, ZERO of 72 lives crossed the individual threshold, and yet
  72 of 72 agents had a non-empty z. All of it came from path B.

So the covariance between w and z can be zero not because nothing happened,
but because the same thing happened to everyone. Path B fills the outcome
while removing the between-agent variation that the covariance needs.

CRITICALLY, path B IS NOT EXOGENOUS. The pool collapses because the agents
themselves over-withdraw. The common shock is downstream of the very
behaviour whose inheritance we are studying. This is the part we are least
sure about, and it is why we are asking rather than deciding.


3. THE OPTIONS WE ARE WEIGHING (for context only - do not choose for us)

1. Keep the outcome as it is, and declare that a common shock can equalise
   it.
2. Read the outcome at a different moment, before the crisis can fire.
3. Replace the outcome with a different quantity.
4. Define the outcome on path A only, excluding the increment written by the
   common shock.
5. Keep the outcome whole, but DECOMPOSE the selection term into a
   within-cell (individual) component and a cell-common component, and report
   both.

Option 5 occurred to us late and we do not know whether it has an accepted
form. Options 4 and 5 are the ones our questions are about.


4. CONSTRAINTS - PLEASE DO NOT PROPOSE SOMETHING THAT VIOLATES THESE

If an answer requires violating one of these, that is useful to know, but
please mark it as such rather than presenting it as an available option.

C1. We may not change the agents' decision rule, or inject any trait,
    disposition or strategy text into them. The whole claim of the study is
    that structure emerges from living, not from being told. This forbids
    "make the agents behave differently so the crisis does not fire".
C2. All measurements are deterministic computations. No model-as-judge, no
    human rating.
C3. The outcome definition must be fixed and written down BEFORE the main
    run, and it may not be selected by looking at the difference between
    arms. We are willing to accept a worse-powered design to keep this.
C4. Constants of the environment are not tuned to produce an effect. A
    constant may only be changed if the new value follows from other
    constants by a stated inequality.
C5. Small numbers: 8 agents per arm per generation, 2 selection episodes per
    seed. Any method that needs hundreds of units per group is not available
    to us, though knowing that it is the standard is still useful.


5. THE QUESTIONS

Q1. NAME AND PROCEDURE.
An outcome variable receives contributions from a unit-specific process and
from a shock that hits all units of a group at the same moment with an
identical increment. Is there an accepted name and an accepted procedure for
isolating the unit-specific component? Candidate vocabularies we suspect are
relevant, but which we may be misapplying: time or period fixed effects in
panel data; within-group (group-mean) centering in multilevel models;
partitioning of common environmental variance (often written c-squared) in
quantitative genetics; removal of a common factor in factor-analytic models.
For each that genuinely applies, give the source, the verbatim sentence that
states the procedure, and say what the procedure assumes.

Q2. WHEN IS IT INVALID.
In our system the common shock is CAUSED by the units' own behaviour: the
pool collapses because the agents over-withdraw. Does removing or excluding
such a component constitute conditioning on a post-treatment variable, a
"bad control", or a collider? What criterion distinguishes a legitimate
removal of a common shock from an illegitimate one? We would like the
criterion stated as a testable condition, with its source, not as general
advice.

Q3. DECOMPOSITION INSTEAD OF EXCLUSION.
In selection analysis - the Price equation, multilevel or contextual analysis
of selection, or any equivalent framework - is there a standard decomposition
that separates a within-group component of an outcome from a group-common
component while KEEPING both, rather than discarding one? If such a
decomposition exists, what is it called, what does it assume, and what is
reported from it? If it exists but is known to be unreliable at our group
size (8 units, 2 episodes), please say so and give the source for that
limitation as well.

Q4. REDEFINING AN OUTCOME AFTER A PILOT.
We ran a pilot. The pilot did not tell us the size of any effect - we have
not looked at the arm contrast - but it did reveal WHICH MECHANISM writes the
outcome. Is redefining a primary outcome on mechanistic grounds discovered in
a pilot an accepted practice, and if so, what conditions must hold for it to
remain legitimate? We are looking for the boundary conditions specifically:
must the pilot data be excluded from the main analysis, must the change be
documented before the main run, and does the reason for the change have to be
independent of any observed effect?

Q5. REPORTING AN OUTCOME THAT COULD NOT VARY.
If we keep the outcome as it is and the common shock equalises it within
every group, our selection term will be zero by construction. Is there an
established way to report "this outcome could not vary, therefore this
analysis is uninformative" that is distinguishable from "we measured no
selection"? We are looking for a named practice - a positive control, a
degenerate-outcome declaration, an estimability or identifiability check
performed before the analysis - with a source.
