Methodology question about experimental design. Do not predict my results
and do not write code.

SETUP. An agent-based evolution experiment. Every lineage is measured at the
same fixed event ordinal L=10, chosen so that it falls at the end of a grace
window during which death is suspended - so every lineage reaches the
measurement point and nothing is censored. Observed lifespans are 11 to 20
events. The mechanism I want to observe (competition for a depleting shared
resource) does not engage until ordinal 17 under current constants. I can
move the mechanism earlier only by changing one environment constant.

Q1. Fixed-age versus fixed-state measurement. In experimental evolution and
life-history studies, how is it handled when the mechanism of interest
engages after the standard measurement age? What is the accepted vocabulary
for measuring at a fixed age versus at a fixed physiological state or event?
If measuring later means some individuals have died, what is the standard
treatment when that death is INFORMATIVE, i.e. correlated with the very
quantity being measured? Is there a defensible way to keep a fixed-age
primary endpoint and still capture a mechanism that engages after it - for
example a fixed-age primary plus a post-onset rate as a declared secondary?

Q2. Choosing a parameter so a mechanism is in range, without it counting as
tuning. I would set the environment constant so that the mechanism engages
inside the observation window. The value follows from an inequality over
constants declared before any run, with no data entering - but it is still
chosen so the mechanism fires. Is there an accepted term and practice for
this: operating point selection, design for detectability, manipulation
check, positive control? Where do reporting guidelines draw the line between
"parameter set so the mechanism is active" and "parameter fitted to the
outcome"? Are there worked examples of a pre-registration or simulation
protocol that declares such a choice explicitly, and how is it justified?

Q3. For a selection differential written as a covariance between relative
reproductive success and a trait (Price equation form), what is known about
estimability and bias at small population size and few episodes of
selection? Is there measured guidance on the minimum number of selection
episodes before the estimate is interpretable? I am NOT asking what effect
size I will get - I am asking what makes the estimate defined rather than
degenerate.

FORMAT, per claim: the claim in one sentence; author, year, DOI or arXiv id;
the section name AND a short verbatim quotation - NOT line numbers, they do
not survive between copies; and whether the evidence is measured, simulated
or theoretical. Attach a bibliography for every reference marker you use. If
you cannot locate a claim in its source, write "cannot show where this
appears": I open every DOI through Crossref and discard unlocatable claims.
